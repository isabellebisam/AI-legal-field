The Regulatory Landscape for AI

As AI systems proliferate across legal, commercial, and social domains, governments worldwide are developing regulatory frameworks to govern their development and deployment. This section examines key regulations affecting AI in legal practice, focusing on four major frameworks with global influence.

[Timeline: Evolution of AI Regulation - 2016: GDPR → 2018: LGPD → 2020: Brazil PL 21/20 → 2021: EU AI Act Proposal → 2023: US AI Executive Order → 2024: EU AI Act Enacted → 2024: Brazil PL 2338 Progress]

1. EU AI Act

Overview

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act, enacted in 2024, is the world's first comprehensive AI regulation. It establishes a risk-based framework for AI systems operating in the European Union, with obligations proportional to potential harm.

Risk-Based Classification

The AI Act categorizes AI systems into four risk levels:

Unacceptable Risk (Prohibited)

Examples: Social scoring by governments, real-time biometric identification in public spaces (with limited exceptions), manipulative AI exploiting vulnerabilities

Consequence: Banned from the EU market

High Risk (Strict Requirements)

Examples: AI in critical infrastructure, employment decisions, law enforcement, biometric identification, credit scoring, educational access, migration/asylum decisions

Requirements: Risk assessment, data governance, documentation, human oversight, accuracy/robustness standards, transparency, cybersecurity measures

Limited Risk (Transparency Obligations)

Examples: Chatbots, deepfakes, emotion recognition systems

Requirements: Users must be informed they're interacting with AI; generated or manipulated content must be disclosed

Minimal Risk (No Specific Obligations)

Examples: Spam filters, AI-enabled video games, recommendation systems (unless high-risk)

Requirements: Voluntary adherence to codes of conduct

General-Purpose AI Models (GPAIs)

The AI Act includes specific provisions for large generative models:

  • All GPAIs: Technical documentation, copyright compliance, training data summaries
  • GPAIs with systemic risk (e.g., GPT-4, Claude): Additional obligations including model evaluations, adversarial testing, incident reporting, cybersecurity measures

Enforcement and Penalties

Violations can result in fines up to:

  • €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover (whichever is higher) for prohibited AI
  • €15 million or 3% of turnover for non-compliance with obligations
  • €7.5 million or 1.5% of turnover for incorrect information

Timeline

  • Prohibited AI bans: 6 months after entry into force
  • GPAI obligations: 12 months after entry into force
  • High-risk AI requirements: 24-36 months after entry into force

Relevance to Legal Practice

The AI Act directly impacts:

  • Legal AI tools: Contract analysis, legal research, document automation may be high-risk if used for consequential decisions
  • Copyright enforcement: Automated takedown systems must meet transparency and human oversight requirements
  • Risk assessment: AI in bail, sentencing, or case outcome prediction classified as high-risk
  • Compliance burden: Law firms and legal tech companies must assess their AI systems' risk levels

2. GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)

Overview

While not AI-specific, the GDPR (enforced since 2018) significantly impacts AI systems through its provisions on automated decision-making and data processing.

Article 22: Automated Decision-Making

Core Principle: Individuals have the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal effects or similarly significantly affect them.

Exceptions: Automated decisions are permissible if:

  • Necessary for entering or performing a contract
  • Authorized by EU or Member State law with appropriate safeguards
  • Based on the individual's explicit consent

Safeguards Required: When automated decisions are permitted, individuals have rights to:

  • Obtain human intervention
  • Express their point of view
  • Contest the decision
  • Obtain an explanation of the decision

The "Right to Explanation" Debate

Article 22 is often cited as creating a "right to explanation" for AI decisions. However:

  • Narrow interpretation: Right to explanation applies only to Article 22 scenarios (solely automated, significant effects)
  • Broad interpretation: Combined with transparency obligations (Articles 13-15), GDPR requires meaningful information about AI logic
  • Practical challenge: Complex AI models (deep learning) may be inherently difficult to explain

Data Protection Principles and AI

Beyond Article 22, GDPR's core principles constrain AI:

  • Purpose limitation: Data collected for one purpose cannot be repurposed for AI training without legal basis
  • Data minimization: AI systems should use only necessary data, not indiscriminate collection
  • Accuracy: AI training data must be accurate and up-to-date
  • Storage limitation: Training data cannot be retained indefinitely without justification
  • Security: AI systems must protect personal data against unauthorized access or breach

Special Categories of Data

Article 9 prohibits processing sensitive data (race, ethnicity, health, biometrics, etc.) without explicit consent or specific legal grounds. AI systems using such data face heightened scrutiny.

GDPR and AI Training Data

Key tensions:

  • Does web scraping for AI training comply with GDPR's lawful basis requirements?
  • Can individuals exercise "right to be forgotten" (Article 17) for data in AI training sets?
  • How does GDPR apply to AI models trained on European data but deployed globally?

Enforcement

GDPR fines can reach €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. Notable AI-related enforcement:

  • Clearview AI fined for biometric data collection without consent
  • Ongoing investigations into AI training data practices

3. Brazil's AI Legislation (PL 2338/2023)

Overview

Brazil is developing comprehensive AI regulation through Projeto de Lei (PL) 2338/2023, building on its existing data protection framework (LGPD - Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados).

Key Features of PL 2338

Risk-Based Framework

Similar to the EU AI Act, PL 2338 categorizes AI systems by risk level (excessive risk, high risk, low risk) with corresponding obligations.

Governance Structure

Proposes creating regulatory authorities and oversight mechanisms specific to AI, coordinated with existing data protection authority (ANPD).

Rights and Responsibilities

Establishes rights for individuals affected by AI decisions, including transparency, explanation, and contestation rights similar to GDPR.

Sandboxes and Innovation

Includes provisions for regulatory sandboxes to encourage responsible AI innovation while testing compliance.

Excessive Risk AI (Prohibited)

PL 2338 proposes banning AI systems that:

  • Manipulate behavior to cause harm
  • Exploit vulnerabilities of specific groups
  • Perform social scoring by public authorities
  • Use subliminal techniques beyond consciousness

High-Risk AI

Systems in critical sectors require:

  • Impact assessments before deployment
  • Human oversight and intervention capabilities
  • Transparency documentation
  • Incident reporting mechanisms
  • Regular audits and monitoring

Critical sectors include: public services, education, employment, credit, law enforcement, judicial systems, critical infrastructure

Connection to LGPD

Brazil's data protection law (LGPD, effective 2020) already addresses some AI issues:

  • Article 20: Right to review automated decisions affecting data subjects
  • Data processing principles applicable to AI training data
  • ANPD (data protection authority) has issued guidance on AI and algorithms

PL 2338 is designed to complement LGPD, providing AI-specific governance while building on data protection foundations.

Status and Timeline

As of 2024, PL 2338 is under legislative review. The bill reflects Brazil's approach of balancing innovation with regulation, drawing lessons from GDPR and the EU AI Act while adapting to Brazilian legal and social contexts.

Significance for Latin America

Brazil's AI legislation may influence other Latin American countries, similar to how LGPD inspired regional data protection laws. It represents a growing trend of Global South nations asserting regulatory sovereignty over AI governance.

4. US Copyright Office AI Guidance

Overview

Unlike the EU and Brazil, the United States has not enacted comprehensive AI regulation. Instead, AI governance emerges through sector-specific rules, agency guidance, and case law. The US Copyright Office has been particularly active in addressing AI and intellectual property.

Key Copyright Office Positions

Human Authorship Requirement (2023)

Position: Copyright protection requires human authorship. Works created solely by AI without human creative input cannot be registered.

Basis: Copyright Act's reference to "author" presumes human creativity, supported by Supreme Court precedent and legislative history.

Impact: Purely AI-generated content lacks copyright protection in the US.

AI-Assisted Works (2023)

Position: Works created with AI assistance may be copyrightable if they contain sufficient human authorship.

Analysis: Copyright Office examines:

  • How the AI tool operates
  • How the human used the tool
  • What elements reflect human creative choices

Example: In Zarya of the Dawn, text and arrangement copyrightable, but individual Midjourney images are not.

Disclosure Requirements (2023)

Position: Copyright applicants must disclose AI-generated content in their works.

Requirement: Application must identify which portions were created by AI and which by humans.

Consequence: Failure to disclose can result in registration refusal or cancellation.

Ongoing Copyright Office Initiatives

The Copyright Office is studying broader AI issues:

  • Notice of Inquiry (2023): Requested public comments on copyright and AI, including training data, outputs, and liability
  • Planned reports: Copyright Office intends to publish comprehensive guidance on AI and copyright law
  • Legislative recommendations: May propose statutory changes to address AI-specific challenges

Other US AI Regulatory Developments

Executive Order on AI (October 2023)

President Biden's executive order on AI safety and security includes:

  • Safety testing requirements for powerful AI models
  • Standards for AI security and privacy
  • Bias and discrimination mitigation in government AI use
  • Transparency and disclosure requirements
Sector-Specific Regulation
  • FTC: Enforcement against deceptive AI claims and unfair practices
  • EEOC: Guidance on AI in employment decisions and discrimination law
  • NIST: AI Risk Management Framework (voluntary standards)
  • State laws: New York (bias audits for hiring AI), California (deepfake regulation), Illinois (biometric privacy)
Proposed Federal Legislation

Multiple AI bills introduced in Congress, including:

  • Algorithmic Accountability Act (requiring impact assessments)
  • AI Training Act (labeling AI-generated content)
  • CREATE AI Act (copyright and licensing frameworks)

Status: As of 2024, no comprehensive federal AI law has been enacted. US regulation remains fragmented across agencies and states.

Comparative Analysis

Key Differences Across Jurisdictions

Regulatory Philosophy
  • EU: Comprehensive, precautionary, risk-based regulation with significant penalties
  • Brazil: Balanced approach combining innovation support with rights protection
  • US: Sector-specific, market-driven, with lighter-touch regulation and case-by-case adjudication
Scope
  • EU AI Act: Broad coverage of AI systems with risk-based obligations
  • GDPR: Data protection focused, applies to AI using personal data
  • Brazil PL 2338: Comprehensive AI regulation complementing data protection law
  • US: Copyright-focused guidance; no comprehensive AI statute
Enforcement Mechanisms
  • EU: High fines (up to 7% of global revenue), centralized enforcement
  • Brazil: Administrative penalties, potential criminal liability (under development)
  • US: Agency actions, private litigation, state enforcement (varies)
Individual Rights
  • EU (GDPR + AI Act): Explanation, contestation, human review rights
  • Brazil (LGPD + PL 2338): Similar rights to EU, with local adaptations
  • US: Limited general rights; sector-specific protections (employment, housing, credit)

Global Trends and Future Directions

Convergence and Divergence

Converging elements:

  • Risk-based approaches to AI regulation
  • Focus on transparency and explainability
  • Human oversight requirements for high-risk systems
  • Restrictions on certain AI uses (social scoring, manipulation)

Diverging elements:

  • Enforcement mechanisms and penalty structures
  • Balance between innovation and precaution
  • Sectoral vs. comprehensive regulation
  • Intellectual property treatment of AI-generated works

Other Jurisdictions

  • China: Sectoral regulations for algorithms, deepfakes, generative AI; emphasis on content control and national security
  • UK: Pro-innovation approach with sector-specific regulation; proposed AI White Paper model
  • Canada: AIDA (Artificial Intelligence and Data Act) proposes risk-based framework within broader privacy legislation
  • Singapore: Model AI Governance Framework (voluntary); sectoral regulation for specific AI applications

International Coordination

Efforts toward global AI governance include:

  • OECD AI Principles: Intergovernmental standards for trustworthy AI
  • UNESCO Recommendation on AI Ethics: Global ethical framework
  • G7 Hiroshima AI Process: Coordination on generative AI governance
  • UN discussions: Proposals for international AI governance institutions

Challenges include sovereignty concerns, competitive dynamics, and differing values across legal traditions.

Implications for Legal Practice

The evolving regulatory landscape requires legal professionals to:

  1. Understand multi-jurisdictional compliance: AI systems often operate globally but face varying legal requirements
  2. Anticipate regulatory change: AI law is rapidly developing; today's guidance may be tomorrow's statute
  3. Advise on risk classification: Determining whether an AI system is high-risk has significant compliance implications
  4. Navigate IP uncertainties: Copyright, patent, and trademark issues for AI-generated works remain unsettled
  5. Implement governance frameworks: Proactive AI governance can mitigate regulatory risk and litigation exposure

As AI becomes embedded in legal practice itself—through research tools, contract analysis, predictive analytics—lawyers must grapple with these regulations not only for clients but for their own professional conduct.
