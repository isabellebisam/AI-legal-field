Glossary

Key terms and concepts related to AI and legal practice, organized alphabetically for quick reference.

Algorithm

A step-by-step procedure or formula for solving a problem or completing a task. In computing, algorithms are implemented as code that processes inputs to produce outputs. AI systems use sophisticated algorithms that can learn and adapt from data.

Algorithmic Bias

Systematic and repeatable errors in AI systems that create unfair outcomes for certain groups. Bias can enter through training data, design choices, or deployment contexts, often perpetuating historical discrimination.

Automated Decision-Making (ADM)

Use of technology to make decisions with limited or no human intervention. In legal contexts, ADM systems may determine copyright enforcement, content moderation, risk assessment, or contract analysis. GDPR Article 22 regulates ADM affecting individuals.

Black Box

An AI system whose internal decision-making process is opaque or incomprehensible, even to its creators. Deep neural networks often function as black boxes, making it difficult to explain specific outputs or identify sources of error.

Content ID

YouTube's automated copyright enforcement system that scans uploaded videos against a database of copyrighted content. Rights holders can choose to block, monetize, or track matching content. Content ID processes hundreds of millions of videos without human review.

Dataset

A collection of data used to train, validate, or test machine learning models. Dataset quality, diversity, and representativeness significantly impact AI performance and fairness. Biased or incomplete datasets lead to biased AI systems.

Deepfake

Synthetic media created using AI, typically depicting people saying or doing things they never did. Deepfakes raise concerns about misinformation, defamation, right of publicity, and authentication of evidence.

Derivative Work

Under copyright law, a new work based on one or more preexisting works. Examples include translations, adaptations, and remixes. Copyright owners have the exclusive right to create or authorize derivative works. AI-generated modifications of copyrighted content may constitute derivative works.

Explainable AI (XAI)

AI systems designed to provide human-understandable explanations for their decisions. XAI techniques include feature importance, counterfactual explanations, and rule extraction. Important for legal accountability, especially under GDPR's transparency requirements.

Fair Use

US copyright doctrine permitting limited use of copyrighted material without permission for purposes like criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, or research. Fair use analysis considers: (1) purpose and character of use, (2) nature of copyrighted work, (3) amount used, (4) effect on market. Fair use is fact-specific and often difficult for AI to assess accurately.

False Negative

An error where a system fails to detect something that exists. In copyright enforcement, a false negative occurs when infringing content is not flagged, allowing infringement to continue unchecked.

False Positive

An error where a system incorrectly identifies something that doesn't exist. In copyright enforcement, a false positive occurs when legitimate content (fair use, original work, licensed content) is incorrectly flagged as infringing, potentially suppressing lawful speech.

GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)

European Union regulation (effective 2018) governing personal data processing. Key provisions affecting AI include Article 22 (automated decision-making rights), data protection principles, and requirements for transparency, accuracy, and security. Violations can result in fines up to €20 million or 4% of global turnover.

Generative AI

AI systems that create new content—text, images, audio, video, or code—rather than merely classifying or analyzing existing content. Examples include GPT (text), DALL-E and Stable Diffusion (images), and Sora (video). Raises unique IP questions about training data, authorship, and output ownership.

Hallucination

When generative AI produces convincing but factually incorrect or nonsensical outputs. Particularly concerning in legal contexts where AI might cite non-existent cases, fabricate legal principles, or generate plausible-sounding but erroneous contract language.

Large Language Model (LLM)

AI models trained on vast text corpora to understand and generate human-like language. Examples include GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini. Used for legal research, contract drafting, document summarization, and client communication. Concerns include hallucinations, copyright issues with training data, and professional responsibility.

Machine Learning (ML)

A subset of AI focused on systems that learn from data without explicit programming for every scenario. ML encompasses supervised learning (learning from labeled examples), unsupervised learning (finding patterns in unlabeled data), and reinforcement learning (learning through trial and error with rewards).

Neural Network

A machine learning architecture inspired by biological neural networks in the brain. Consists of layers of interconnected nodes (neurons) that process and transform data. Deep neural networks (with many layers) power modern AI achievements but are often difficult to interpret.

Overfitting

When a machine learning model learns training data too specifically, including noise and outliers, reducing its ability to generalize to new data. Results in high training accuracy but poor real-world performance.

Platform Governance

Rules, processes, and systems by which digital platforms moderate content, enforce policies, and manage user behavior. Increasingly automated through AI. Raises questions about private power, free expression, due process, and democratic accountability.

Predictive Policing

Use of AI to forecast where crimes might occur or who might commit them, guiding law enforcement resource allocation. Controversial due to bias concerns—models trained on historical arrest data may perpetuate discriminatory policing patterns.

Reinforcement Learning

Machine learning approach where systems learn through trial and error, receiving rewards or penalties for actions. Used to optimize complex decision-making processes. In legal contexts, could optimize content moderation strategies or settlement negotiations.

Risk Assessment (Algorithmic)

Use of AI to predict likelihood of future events, particularly in criminal justice (recidivism, bail decisions, sentencing). Tools like COMPAS and PSA have been criticized for racial bias and lack of transparency. Classified as high-risk AI under the EU AI Act.

Supervised Learning

Machine learning approach using labeled training data where each example includes the desired output. The model learns to map inputs to outputs. For example, copyright detection systems learn from examples labeled as "infringing" or "non-infringing."

Training Data

Data used to teach machine learning models. Quality, diversity, and legal status of training data are critical. Generative AI controversy centers on use of copyrighted works as training data without permission. Biased training data produces biased AI systems.

Transparency (Algorithmic)

The degree to which AI systems' functioning is open to inspection and understanding. Includes transparency about: (1) how the system works generally, (2) why specific decisions were made, (3) what data was used. Essential for accountability and due process. Required by GDPR and EU AI Act in many contexts.

Turing Test

Proposed by Alan Turing in 1950 as a measure of machine intelligence: if a human evaluator cannot distinguish machine responses from human responses, the machine exhibits intelligent behavior. Modern LLMs can often pass Turing tests in specific contexts, though this doesn't mean they truly "understand" in human terms.

Unsupervised Learning

Machine learning using unlabeled data, where the system finds patterns, groupings, or structures without pre-defined categories. Used for clustering similar legal documents, detecting anomalies in contracts, or discovering patterns in case law.

Validation (Model)

The process of evaluating a trained machine learning model's performance on data it hasn't seen before. Essential for assessing whether the model will generalize to real-world use rather than just memorizing training data.
